# Philosophy

What this atlas is for, and how to decide what belongs in it. Written from the owner's words on 2026-11-19. When a design choice is unclear, decide it by this page.

## The aim

A centre of information about the deep human past: every published archaeological site on one globe, with the oldest and strangest of them explored in depth. The reader draws their own conclusions. The atlas puts everything on the table and shows what holds each idea up.

The focus is the very ancient: Anatolia, Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe and the Taş Tepeler first, then the other places that raise the same questions, such as Giza. Turkey comes first; the whole globe is the direction.

## Principles

1. **Ideas are welcome. Evidence ranks them.** No theory is refused a place for being unusual. Each one stands as high as its evidence and argument hold it.
2. **A theory is downgraded, never deleted.** When evidence or experiment goes against a theory it moves down, with the reason and the date recorded. With no evidence and no argument at all it is shelved, awaiting evidence. It can move back up.
3. **Facts, readings and theories are kept apart.** A measurement or an excavated find is a fact. What it means is a reading, and a reading has an author. A theory connects many facts and readings. The reader can always tell which of the three they are looking at.
4. **Every claim has an owner and a source.** "Schmidt read this as…", "Hancock proposes…", "the excavators dispute…". The atlas reports who says what. It does not speak in its own voice about what is true.
5. **Questions are worth showing.** "How were blocks of this weight moved?" is a good entry even with no answer. An honest "not known" is better than a tidy story.
6. **A puzzle is a claim to test, not a premise.** "The technology of the time cannot explain this" goes on the table beside the experiments and finds that bear on it, for and against.
7. **Show the counter-evidence with the same care.** Each theory lists what supports it, what goes against it, and what would settle it.
8. **No conclusions on the reader's behalf.** The atlas does not say a site was built by a lost civilisation, and it does not say the idea is nonsense. It shows the case as it stands.
9. **The published record stays clean.** Theories live in their own layer and their own records. They never change a site's published date, type or description, and they are never drawn into a reconstruction as if excavated.
10. **Nothing that puts a site at risk.** No precise locations of unpublished or candidate sites, ever. The looting rules in `AGENTS.md` come before everything on this page.

## The status ladder

Every theory carries one of these, with the date it was last changed and why.

| Status | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Well supported | Several independent lines of evidence; survives the known objections. |
| Contested | Real evidence on both sides; specialists disagree. |
| Weakly supported | Some evidence or a coherent argument; serious gaps or objections. |
| Unsupported so far | An idea with an argument but no evidence yet found for it. |
| Shelved | No evidence and no argument to examine. Kept on record, awaiting evidence. |

"Fringe" and "mainstream" are not statuses. Who proposes an idea is recorded; where it stands depends on the evidence listed under it.

## What this means when building

- A reconstruction shows what is published. Anything by eye is marked estimated. Anything conjectural is a labelled layer, off by default, naming whose hypothesis it is.
- A site card may link to theories. A theory card always shows its status, its evidence for and against, and its sources.
- Wording stays level: no "debunked", no "proves", no "mystery solved". Say who found what, and how strong it is.
- Adding a theory needs its proponent, at least one source for the claim itself, and an honest first status. It does not need to be believed.
- Adding evidence against a popular idea is as welcome as adding evidence for an unpopular one.
